The Indian national calendar (Saka calendar)

India has an official civil calendar of its own, based on the Saka era, used by the government alongside the common calendar.

What it is

The Indian national calendar, often called the Saka calendar, is the official civil calendar of the Republic of India. It came into use on 1 Chaitra 1879 Saka, which was 22 March 1957. It is used together with the common (Gregorian) calendar, not in place of it.

Its years are numbered in the Saka era, whose starting point corresponds to the year 78 of the common era. To turn a common year into a Saka year, subtract 78 for dates after the new year in March and 79 for dates before it.

Where you see it

  • The Gazette of India, which prints the Saka date beside the common date on every issue.
  • News broadcasts of All India Radio.
  • Calendars issued by the Government of India.
  • Government communications addressed to the public, and official orders such as the yearly list of Central Government holidays, which gives every holiday's Saka date.

In daily life, business and schooling, the common calendar is what everyone uses, and festivals are fixed by the traditional regional calendars. The national calendar's role is official and symbolic.

Why it was made

At Independence, India used about thirty different calendars for religious and civil purposes. They disagreed with one another about new year's day, about the lengths of months and even about the dates of festivals. In 1952 the Government of India set up the Calendar Reform Committee, with the astrophysicist Meghnad Saha as chairman, to study them and recommend one accurate, uniform calendar for the whole country. The committee reported in 1955, and its civil calendar was adopted from 1957.

The committee's design kept familiar month names but fixed the lengths of the months and tied the year firmly to the Sun, so that it would never drift against the seasons.

How the year is built

  • The year begins on 1 Chaitra, the day after the spring equinox: 22 March, or 21 March in a leap year.
  • Chaitra has 30 days, or 31 in a leap year.
  • The next five months, Vaisakha, Jyaishtha, Ashadha, Sravana and Bhadra, have 31 days each.
  • The last six, Asvina, Kartika, Agrahayana, Pausha, Magha and Phalguna, have 30 days each.
  • A year is a leap year when the common year in which it begins is a leap year. So leap days in the two calendars always fall in the same twelve-month stretch.

The five long months sit in the half of the year when the Earth is farthest from the Sun and moves most slowly along its orbit, so the Sun takes longer to pass through that part of the sky. The table of start dates is on the Saka date converter page, which also converts any date both ways.

Reading an official date

The 2027 holiday list of the Central Government shows Diwali as "October 29, Kartika 07, Friday". Kartika begins on 23 October, so 29 October is the 7th of Kartika. The Saka year is 2027 − 78 = 1949. The full date is 7 Kartika 1949.

National calendar and traditional calendars

The names Chaitra, Vaisakha and the rest are shared with the traditional Hindu calendars, and this causes confusion. The differences are large.

So "1 Chaitra" of the national calendar is a fixed date in March, while the Chaitra of the panchang, in which Ugadi, Gudi Padwa and the Navratri of spring fall, begins on a different March or April date every year.

Other eras in use

India's regional calendars count years in several eras. Vikram Samvat, common in north and west India and the official calendar of Nepal, runs about 57 years ahead of the common era. The Bengali, Tamil, Malayalam (Kollam) and Nanakshahi calendars have their own reckonings, and the Islamic Hijri calendar is purely lunar. This site converts only the national calendar, because it is the one with a fixed, published rule.

When does the Saka year begin?

On 22 March, or on 21 March when the common year is a leap year.

Which Saka year corresponds to 2027?

Saka 1948 up to 21 March 2027, and Saka 1949 from 22 March 2027.

Is the national calendar used to fix festival dates?

No. Festivals follow the traditional lunisolar calendars. The national calendar is used for official dating.
