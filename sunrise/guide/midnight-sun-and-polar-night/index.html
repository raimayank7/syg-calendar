Midnight Sun and Polar Night

Places north of the Arctic Circle and south of the Antarctic Circle have days with no sunset and days with no sunrise. How many, and why.

What happens

The earth's axis is tilted about 23.4°. In summer the pole tilts towards the sun, and a region around it never turns out of the light during a full day: the midnight sun. In winter the same region never turns into the light: the polar night. The Arctic and Antarctic Circles, at latitude 66.6°, mark the edge: on the summer solstice the sun just stays up at the circle, and polar night is just reached on the winter solstice.

How many days

The farther towards the pole, the longer. Towards 70° N there are about two months of midnight sun and two months of polar night. At the pole there are about six months of each. The exact count depends on how one defines sunrise, and on atmospheric bending of light, which makes the sun visible when it is slightly below the horizon, so the real period of sunlight is a little longer than a geometric calculation.

On this site

For cities within the polar regions, such as Tromsø or Reykjavik's northern neighbours, the city page counts the days of midnight sun and polar night in 2027, and the daily table shows 24 h or 0 h on those days. Reykjavik itself lies just below the Arctic Circle and has no polar night, but has weeks of light nights in June.

Living with it

People in the far north use blackout curtains in summer and bright lamps in winter. Travellers should know that the "day" on a clock bears little relation to light in these weeks: shops open and close on clock time, and schoolchildren go to bed in full daylight. Photographers like the long golden hours near the edges of the polar day, when the sun skims the horizon for a long time.

Even outside the polar circles the effect is felt. At 60° north, in Oslo or Helsinki, the June night is never fully dark, and in December there are only about six hours of daylight; our ranking page shows the figures for each large city.
