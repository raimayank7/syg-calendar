Reading the table

The two dates in the table are close to the longest and the shortest day of the year in the northern hemisphere; in the southern hemisphere it is the other way round. All of them use the time zone Asia/Taipei.

On 21 June, the longest day among these cities is in New Taipei City (13 h 42 min) and the shortest in Kaohsiung (13 h 31 min), a spread of 11 minutes. On 21 December the longest is in Kaohsiung (10 h 45 min) and the shortest in New Taipei City (10 h 35 min), a spread of 10 minutes. The spread comes from latitude: places further from the equator have a bigger change in daylight during the year.

The northernmost of these cities is New Taipei City at 25.1° and the southernmost Kaohsiung at 22.6°, a difference of 2.4 degrees of latitude (about 271 km). Each degree of latitude changes the length of the longest day by a few minutes in temperate regions, which is why the two ends of a long country do not share the same daylight.

Taipei lies 1.3° east of Tainan while both keep the same clock, so the sun rises about 5 minutes earlier by the clock in Taipei on the same date. This is the effect of longitude: four minutes for every degree.

Each city name opens a page with the whole of 2027: a month-by-month summary, every day in a folding list, the longest and shortest days, the earliest and latest sunrise and sunset, and a comparison with the capital or the largest neighbouring city. Dates and times are local clock times, so they include any daylight saving change in force.

The list holds the cities of Taiwan with the largest populations in the GeoNames data, plus its capital. For a smaller town, use the calculator on the home page with the place that is nearest: within about 50 kilometres east or west the clock times differ by less than four minutes, and north or south the day length differs by a minute or less.
