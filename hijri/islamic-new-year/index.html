Hijri Calendar › Holidays

Dates can differ by a day or more. Islamic months begin with the sighting of the new moon, and countries and religious authorities decide differently, so Ramadan, Eid and other dates may be observed one day (sometimes two) later or earlier than shown. The dates on this site are calculated by the Umm al-Qura method, the official civil calendar of Saudi Arabia (King Abdulaziz City for Science and Technology, ummulqura.org.sa). They are an estimate for planning, not an announcement: wait for your own authority's announcement.

What it is

The Hijri year is counted from the Hijra, the journey of the Prophet Muhammad and his companions from Mecca to Medina in 622 CE. The caliph Umar is traditionally said to have chosen that event as year one, and chose Muharram, the month that follows the Hajj season, as the first month. Years are written with the letters AH, from the Latin 'Anno Hegirae'.

Islamic New Year is not a festival with a fixed ceremony. For most Muslims it is a day to reflect on the year gone by, to make plans for the next, and to remember the Hijra, a story of leaving home for the sake of faith. Greeting cards and short messages are common in many countries, and in some, such as Saudi Arabia, the date is not a public holiday at all, while in others, such as Malaysia and Indonesia, it is.

Because the Islamic year is a lunar one of 354 or 355 days, the new year arrives about ten or eleven days earlier on the Gregorian calendar each year. It slowly moves round all seasons in about 33 years. The date below follows the Umm al-Qura table; the day you observe depends on your country's moon sighting.

How the date moves

In 2026 it falls on 16 June and in 2035 on 11 March, so across ten years it drifts through the Gregorian year, about ten or eleven days earlier every year, and now and then jumps back by about three weeks when the calendar catches up.

Over these ten years the weekday count is: Sunday (3), Wednesday (2), Tuesday (1), Thursday (1), Monday (1), Saturday (1), Friday (1). A lunar year has 354 or 355 days, 11 or 10 days fewer than a Gregorian one, so no observance keeps a Gregorian date. It returns to the same season about every 33 years.

Plan around uncertainty

Treat the date as the earliest likely one. If you must book travel, leave from work, or schedule an exam, wait for the announcement in your own country, usually the evening before; moon sighting can shift the date by a day, and for some countries the observance follows Saudi Arabia, while for others it follows local sighting.
