Dates can differ by a day or more. Islamic months begin with the sighting of the new moon, and countries and religious authorities decide differently, so Ramadan, Eid and other dates may be observed one day (sometimes two) later or earlier than shown. The dates on this site are calculated by the Umm al-Qura method, the official civil calendar of Saudi Arabia (King Abdulaziz City for Science and Technology, ummulqura.org.sa). They are an estimate for planning, not an announcement: wait for your own authority's announcement.

What it is

Ramadan is the ninth month and the month of the fast, one of the five pillars of Islam. From the first light of dawn until sunset, adult Muslims who are well, not travelling and not menstruating or in the period after childbirth do not eat, drink or smoke; children, the elderly, the sick, travellers and pregnant or nursing women who find it hard are excused and make up days later or feed a poor person.

A meal before dawn, suhoor, and the meal that ends the fast, iftar, which is traditionally opened with dates and water, shape the day. At night Muslims pray the tarawih prayers in the mosque, many recite the Qur'an through the month, and acts of charity are increased. The last ten nights are held especially dear, and Laylat al-Qadr is sought among them.

The month has 29 or 30 days. Its start depends on the sighting of the new moon in most countries, and in countries following the Umm al-Qura calendar on a calculation, so neighbouring countries may begin on different days. Day length varies a great deal with place and season; in the years when Ramadan falls in summer the fast in northern countries is much longer than in winter.

How the date moves

In 2026 it falls on 18 February and in 2035 on 1 November, so across ten years it drifts through the Gregorian year, about ten or eleven days earlier every year, and now and then jumps back by about three weeks when the calendar catches up.

Over these ten years the weekday count is: Wednesday (2), Tuesday (2), Saturday (2), Thursday (2), Monday (1), Friday (1), Sunday (1). A lunar year has 354 or 355 days, 11 or 10 days fewer than a Gregorian one, so no observance keeps a Gregorian date. It returns to the same season about every 33 years.

Plan around uncertainty

Treat the date as the earliest likely one. If you must book travel, leave from work, or schedule an exam, wait for the announcement in your own country, usually the evening before; moon sighting can shift the date by a day, and for some countries the observance follows Saudi Arabia, while for others it follows local sighting.
