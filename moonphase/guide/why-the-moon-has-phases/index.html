Why the moon has phases

The moon makes no light of its own. Half of it is always lit by the sun, and the phase is how much of that lit half we can see from Earth.

The moon is a ball of rock that shines only by reflecting sunlight. At any moment the half facing the sun is lit and the other half is dark. As the moon travels once round the Earth in about a month, we look at that lit half from changing angles. When the moon is between the Earth and the sun, the lit side faces away and we see almost nothing: the new moon. A week later we see half of the lit side, a first quarter. When the Earth is between the sun and the moon the whole lit side faces us: the full moon. A week after that comes the last quarter.

The eight names

  • New moon: moon and sun in the same direction, the moon is not visible.
  • Waxing crescent: a thin curve of light growing each evening, low in the west after sunset.
  • First quarter: half the disc lit, high in the sky at sunset.
  • Waxing gibbous: more than half lit, growing.
  • Full moon: the whole disc lit, rising at sunset and setting at sunrise.
  • Waning gibbous: more than half lit, shrinking, rising after dark.
  • Last quarter: half the disc lit again, rising around midnight.
  • Waning crescent: a thin curve before dawn.

Why the cycle takes 29.5 days

The moon goes round the Earth in about 27.3 days, but meanwhile the Earth moves along its own orbit round the sun, so the moon needs about two more days to line up with the sun again. That gap between two new moons, the synodic month, averages 29.53 days and varies between about 29.3 and 29.8 days because the orbits are not circles. This is why the interval between two full moons on the tables on this site is sometimes 29 and sometimes 30 days.

Common mistakes

The phases are not caused by the Earth's shadow; that happens only in a lunar eclipse. The moon does not change shape: the same face points to us all the time. And the same phase looks different from the northern and southern hemispheres: the crescent that looks like a "D" from Delhi looks like a "C" from Sydney, because the viewer is upside down relative to the other.
