Why a Festival Can Be on Different Days in Different Countries

Eid, Diwali and other festivals are observed on different days in different places. Moon sighting, time zones and local traditions explain it.

Moon sighting

For Islamic festivals, many communities start a month when a thin new crescent moon is actually seen. Where the sky is clear the crescent may be seen a day before it is seen elsewhere. Saudi Arabia uses a calculated calendar for civil dates, but the start of Ramadan and the Eids is announced after sighting. Countries in South Asia usually announce a day later than Saudi Arabia. The same Eid can be on a Thursday in one country and on a Friday in another.

Time zones and the day line

A new moon happens at a single moment worldwide, but the date of that moment depends on the time zone. A festival tied to a moon phase can therefore be on a different date for people far apart, for example in New Zealand and in the United States.

Local tradition

In India the panchang (almanac) used in a region decides which day is the festival day: some use the lunar day at sunrise, some at midday or at night. For Diwali, the day of the new moon at night is chosen, and in some years the new moon spans two dates. Regional calendars (Tamil, Bengali, Malayalam) have their own new-year days and festival dates, some fixed to the sun.

What to do

Use the date on a page like this for planning, then confirm with your own community, temple, mosque, synagogue or government notice before you act on it.
