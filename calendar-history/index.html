Where our calendar came from

The calendar on your wall is Roman in its bones, was repaired by Julius Caesar, and was corrected once more in 1582.

From Rome's ten months to twelve

The earliest Roman calendar that tradition records had ten months and began in March. Its last four months were simply numbered, and the names survive: September, October, November and December come from the Latin words for seven, eight, nine and ten. They stopped matching their numbers when January and February were added and, later, the start of the year was moved to January.

The Roman year was shorter than the year of the seasons, so priests added an extra month from time to time. They did it irregularly, sometimes for political reasons, and by the time of Julius Caesar the calendar was about three months out of step with the seasons.

Julius Caesar's reform

Advised by the astronomer Sosigenes of Alexandria, Caesar ordered a new calendar that began on 1 January 45 BCE. To pull the dates back into line, the year before it was stretched to 445 days; it is remembered as the "year of confusion". The new Julian calendar had 365 days in twelve months of the lengths we still use, and one extra day every fourth year.

Two months were later renamed in honour of rulers: Quintilis became July for Julius Caesar, and Sextilis became August for Augustus.

The eleven-minute problem

A Julian year averages 365.25 days. The real year of the seasons is about 365.2422 days. The difference, about eleven minutes a year, grows to a full day every 128 years or so. By the sixteenth century the spring equinox, which had fallen around 21 March when the rules for Easter were settled in the year 325, was arriving on about 11 March.

The Gregorian correction of 1582

Pope Gregory XIII issued a reform worked out by the physician Aloysius Lilius and the mathematician Christopher Clavius. It did two things.

  1. Ten dates were dropped. In the countries that adopted it at once, Thursday 4 October 1582 was followed by Friday 15 October 1582. The run of weekdays was not broken.
  2. The leap rule was refined. Century years would be leap years only if divisible by 400. See the leap year page.

Who changed when

The change spread slowly, because it came from Rome and many Protestant and Orthodox countries distrusted it.

In Britain, Wednesday 2 September 1752 was followed by Thursday 14 September. The same Act moved the start of the legal year in England from 25 March to 1 January. This is why dates in old English records between January and March sometimes carry two years, such as "1701/02".

Because of the staggered change, a date in an old document must be read with its country in mind. The October Revolution in Russia took place in what the rest of Europe already called November.

The calendar today

The Gregorian calendar is now the civil calendar of almost the whole world, used for business, travel and government even where another calendar guides religious life. India uses it side by side with the Indian national calendar. Its average year is still about 27 seconds too long, an error of one day in roughly three thousand years, and no change is planned.

Why did ten days disappear in 1582?

To move the spring equinox back to about 21 March, where it stood when the Easter rules were made. The Julian calendar had drifted by ten days over twelve centuries.

Did people lose ten days of their lives?

No. Only the labels on the days changed. The weekdays ran on without a break.

Why is September the ninth month if its name means seven?

Because the Roman year once began in March, which made September the seventh month.
