About these guides

The calendar is so familiar that its oddities go unnoticed: months of four different lengths, a leap day in the shortest month, a ninth month whose name means "seventh", and a new year in the middle of winter. Each of these has a reason, and the reasons are a good story of astronomy, Roman politics and slow correction over two thousand years.

These guides explain them in plain words. They also cover the things about dates that cause real mistakes at work: the day-first and month-first ways of writing a date, the financial year and its quarters, and the difference between India's official national calendar and the traditional calendars by which festivals are fixed.

Where to start

  • For school projects and quiz preparation: days in each month, where our calendar came from and the leap year page.
  • For office work: financial year and quarters and date formats.
  • For the Indian calendars: the Indian national calendar, then the Saka date converter.
  • For language learners and children: days of the week and months of the year in Hindi and Punjabi, each in its own script with the way it is said.

Each guide ends with the questions people most often ask and links to the calculator that goes with it.

Who writes the guides?

They are our own writing. Facts about the history of the calendar are long-settled and are checked against standard references; dates and weekdays quoted in them are verified by our own calculators.

Can I use the guides for teaching?

Yes. You are welcome to use them in class and to print pages for pupils. Please do not republish the text as your own.
