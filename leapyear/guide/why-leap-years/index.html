Why we have leap years

The Earth takes a little more than 365 days to go around the Sun. Leap years keep the calendar in step with the seasons.

The problem

The time from one March equinox to the next, the tropical year, is about 365.2422 days. A calendar of 365 days falls behind by nearly a quarter of a day each year, which adds up to one day in four years and to the seasons slowly moving through the calendar. Adding a day every four years, as the Julian calendar did from 45 BC, gives a mean year of 365.25 days, which is too long by 0.0078 days, or one day in 128 years.

The Gregorian rule

In 1582 Pope Gregory XIII's calendar dropped three leap days every 400 years: century years are leap years only if divisible by 400. The mean year is then 365.2425 days (97 leap days in 400 years: 365 + 97/400). It is longer than the tropical year by about 0.0003 days, so the calendar gains one day over about 3,300 years. No further correction has been adopted.

Countries that waited

The change from the Julian to the Gregorian calendar was made by different countries at different times, from 1582 in Catholic countries to 1752 in Britain and its colonies, and 1918 to 1923 in Russia and Greece, so dates of the 16th to 20th centuries need a note on the calendar used.
