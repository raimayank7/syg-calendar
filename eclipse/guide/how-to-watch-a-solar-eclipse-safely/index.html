How to watch a solar eclipse safely

The only safe ways to look at the sun during an eclipse: certified glasses, solar filters and pinhole projection. What is not safe.

Looking at the sun without protection can damage the retina, the light-sensing layer at the back of the eye, within seconds, and often without pain. This applies to every partial and annular eclipse, and to the partial phases of a total eclipse, even if the sun is mostly covered.

Safe

  • Eclipse glasses or viewers made to the ISO 12312-2 standard. Check for the standard printed on them, no scratches or holes, and buy from a reputable maker. Do not use them with a telescope or binoculars.
  • A solar filter on the front of a telescope, binoculars or camera lens. A filter at the eyepiece end is not safe.
  • Pinhole projection: let sunlight pass through a small hole in a card and look at the image on a second card or the ground. Leaves of a tree make hundreds of little crescent images.
  • Watching online broadcasts made by observatories.

Not safe

  • Sunglasses, smoked glass, film negatives, CDs, X-ray films or stacked filters.
  • Phone cameras without a proper filter can be damaged, and looking at the screen is fine but looking at the sun through the viewfinder is not.
  • Looking at the sun through a telescope or binoculars without a filter on the front.

Total eclipse

In the narrow path of a total eclipse it is safe to look without protection only during the few minutes when the sun is completely covered, and you must put the glasses back on as soon as the first bright sliver returns. A partial eclipse is never safe to look at with the naked eye.

This is general safety information; follow the instructions of the maker of your glasses and of your local observatory.
