What is in this list

There are 34 solar eclipses from 2026 to 2040: 12 partial, 11 annular, 10 total, 1 hybrid. A solar eclipse can be seen only from a part of the Earth, and a total one only from a narrow path; so for most people the next one they can see is a partial eclipse.

See the guide to how eclipses work and the safety guide.

By type

  • Partial: 12, the next on Sunday, 14 January 2029. In a partial eclipse the moon covers only part of the sun, so nobody on Earth sees a complete eclipse.
  • Annular: 11, the next on Saturday, 6 February 2027. In an annular solar eclipse the moon is too far from Earth to cover the sun completely, so a bright ring of sun ("ring of fire") stays visible along a narrow path; elsewhere it is a partial eclipse.
  • Total: 10, the next on Monday, 2 August 2027. In a total solar eclipse the moon covers the whole sun for a few minutes along a narrow path; outside the path people see a partial eclipse.
  • Hybrid: 1, the next on Friday, 14 November 2031. A hybrid eclipse is annular along part of its path and total along another part, because the curve of the Earth brings the moon's shadow tip closer to the surface in the middle.

How often

Over these 15 years there are 34 solar eclipses, an average of 2.3 a year, and the most in one year is 4 (2029). Eclipses come in seasons about six months apart, because the orbit of the moon is tilted and crosses the line to the sun only twice a year.
