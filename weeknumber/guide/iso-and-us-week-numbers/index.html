ISO and US week numbers

Two ways of counting weeks give different numbers for the same date. Here is how each works and how to get each in a spreadsheet.

Two systems for numbering weeks are in common use. The ISO 8601 system, used in most of Europe, Asia and much business planning, starts weeks on Monday and calls the week that holds the first Thursday of the year week 1. Equivalently, week 1 is the week that has at least four days in the new year, and the week with 4 January in it. A year then has 52 or 53 weeks and the first days of January may belong to week 52 or 53 of the old year, and the last days of December to week 1 of the next.

The US-style system starts weeks on Sunday and calls the week that contains 1 January week 1, even if it holds only one day of the new year. This is the default of the WEEKNUM function in Excel and Google Sheets when no return type is given. The year then has 53 or 54 partial weeks, the first and last of which may be short.

Example

1 January 2027 is a Friday. By the ISO rule it is in week 53 of 2026. By the US rule it is in week 1 of 2027. Both numberings agree for most of a year's dates, but the dates of week boundaries are shifted by a day, and the number can differ by one.

In spreadsheets

Excel and Google Sheets have ISOWEEKNUM(date) for the ISO number and WEEKNUM(date, 21) for the same ISO rule; WEEKNUM(date) or WEEKNUM(date, 1) gives the US-style number. When you share a file with people in other countries, use the ISO functions, and say so.

Other systems

Some countries and companies number weeks differently: some Middle Eastern countries start weeks on Saturday, and many retailers use their own fiscal weeks. Some calendars put the first Monday as week 1. If a number matters, say which system you mean and write the dates too.
