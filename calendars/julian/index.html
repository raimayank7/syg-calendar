The Julian Calendar

The Julian calendar has a leap day every four years with no exception. It runs 13 days behind the Gregorian calendar from 1900 to 2099.

How it works

Julius Caesar introduced the calendar in 45 BC. It has twelve months, the same lengths as the Gregorian months, and adds 29 February every fourth year without exception. Its year is 365.25 days long on average, a little longer than the solar year, so it slowly drifts: the Julian calendar gains about three days every 400 years on the seasons. The Gregorian reform of 1582 dropped three leap days every 400 years to fix this.

The 13 days

From 1 March 1900 to 28 February 2100 the Julian date is 13 days behind the Gregorian date. In 2100 the Gregorian calendar skips 29 February and the Julian calendar does not, so the gap becomes 14 days. The table shows 1 January of the Julian calendar as a Gregorian date for several years. Orthodox Christmas on 25 December in the Julian calendar falls on 7 January in the Gregorian calendar.

Who uses it

Several Orthodox churches (including those of Russia, Serbia and Jerusalem) use the Julian calendar to date Easter and some use it for fixed feasts too. Most countries use the Gregorian calendar for civil life. Russia changed its civil calendar in 1918, Greece in 1923.
