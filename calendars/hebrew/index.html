The Hebrew Calendar

The Hebrew calendar is a lunisolar calendar: months follow the moon and a leap month is added seven times in 19 years to keep the festivals in their seasons.

How it works

A Hebrew year has 12 months in an ordinary year and 13 in a leap year. Seven years out of every 19 are leap years, with an extra month, Adar I, so that Passover always falls in spring. A year has 353, 354 or 355 days, or 383, 384 or 385 days in a leap year, because the months Heshvan and Kislev can have 29 or 30 days to keep Rosh Hashanah off certain weekdays. The count of years starts from the traditional date of creation, so the year that begins in autumn of the Gregorian year Y is Y + 3761.

The months

The year begins in Tishri for the year number, but the months are counted from Nisan in spring: Nisan, Iyyar, Sivan, Tammuz, Av, Elul, Tishri, Heshvan, Kislev, Tevet, Shevat, Adar (Adar I and Adar II in a leap year). A day begins at nightfall of the evening before, so festivals start in the evening of the Gregorian date before the one shown here.

The method

The calendar here is the fixed arithmetic Hebrew calendar, the one used since the fourth century, where the new moon is the mean molad. It is not computed from the real moon.
